The backtest never knows a trade before the public does

The most common error in this kind of analysis is trading on the member's trade date. That date only becomes known when the filing is published. The backtest therefore buys at the earliest at the open on the day after the filing.

  1. The member trades

    The trade date is in the filing, but nobody else knows it yet.

  2. The filing

    The STOCK Act deadline is 45 days. 19% of purchases were filed later.

    Median 28 days; 90% within 226 days

  3. Made public

    The filing date from the House's own index. Only now is the trade known.

  4. The fund buys

    At the next day's opening price, with a trading cost of 0.1%.

From PDF to backtest in five stages

Each stage is a separate Python module with its own tests. All data comes from free, public sources.

Collect

The House's yearly index and every transaction report PDF since 2016. The download can resume after interruptions and failed on 0 of 7,004 files. requests, XML; 19 tests

Parse

Three templates, page breaks in the middle of a trade, lower-case tickers and amended filings. Duplicates and deleted trades are flagged. pypdf, regular expressions; 46 tests

Price

Daily prices adjusted for splits and dividends. Ticker changes are traced through history, and delisted stocks are kept. Alpaca SIP feed; 12 tests

Simulate

136 combinations of skip threshold, exit rule and weighting, chosen on 2016–2021 and then tested unchanged on 2022–2026. pandas, numpy; 42 tests

Review

An independent review in two rounds. The first found two serious errors, which were fixed; the second found none. Newey-West statistics, hand-checked samples

The rules, fixed before the results

The rules were written down before any result was seen. Where a rule was unclear, the most conservative reading was chosen and recorded.

Signal
Every purchase of a stock or ETF, including those of spouses and children. No options, bonds or crypto.
Skip rule
Skip a purchase only if the price has risen more than X% since the member's trade. A fall never causes a skip.
Threshold X
0, 2, 4 … 30%, and no threshold
Exit
When the member sells, or after a fixed 3, 6 or 12 months
Weighting
Equal per trade, or by the midpoint of the disclosed amount range
Benchmark
S&P 500 (SPY with dividends) over the same period
Overfitting guard
Rules chosen on 2016–2021 only, then tested unchanged on 2022 to today
Costs
0.1% on each purchase and each sale

Ten years of results

The rule chosen on 2016–2021 holds each purchase for six months, weights it by the disclosed amount and skips it if the price has already risen more than 10%. Over the full period it returned +400% against +338% for the S&P 500, with more volatility.

What the independent review found

Round 1: two serious errors
The first version showed +106% against +66% for the test period. The review showed this came from the test portfolio starting empty and holding almost only one member's energy purchases in 2022. Measured as one continuous portfolio, the lead disappeared.
Round 2: no serious errors
All 18,068 trades were recomputed from the raw data with no differences, and ten random trades were checked by hand. The choice of rule is fragile: several variants score almost the same.

Following only the best members

The next idea was to copy only members whose earlier purchases had beaten the market, judging each member only on trades that had closed before the purchase. 128 variants were tested, and the best on 2016–2021 was chosen by a rule fixed in advance: copy the top 20% of members over the last 24 months.

Two members made up 75% of the portfolio: Mark Green's energy purchases and some very large Microsoft purchases by Suzan DelBene's spouse. Per trade, the selected members did 1.8 percentage points worse than the market over six months, against 0.9 for those not selected. Past winners did not keep winning. A deflated Sharpe ratio would need to exceed 0.95 to count as evidence.

Trying to reduce the risk

Eighteen fixed variants of known risk techniques were tested on top of the same signal: equal, amount and square-root weighting; caps of 5% per stock or 15% per member; and volatility targeting.

None improved the original rule on 2016–2021, so the live fund keeps it. Volatility targeting lowered the swings (22.9% to 15.5% a year since 2022) but also the return (+63.9% to +52.4%), and it helped the S&P 500 just as much, so it says nothing about this strategy in particular. A 5% cap per stock would have looked better after 2022, but it was worse before, and picking it now would mean choosing with hindsight.

What the data cannot tell us

Gaps are reported as gaps. Nothing is filled in with estimates.

Scanned filings are missing
1,692 of 7,004 filings (24%) are scanned paper without text. They are concentrated in 2016–2018 and among some of the most active traders. Two independent readers agreed on 254 of 254 trades in a sample, but the rest are not in this version.
Survivorship bias
5.2% of purchases have no price at entry, mostly delisted companies. This can flatter the result. The number is reported in every calculation.
Amended filings
324 amended rows cannot be matched to an original and may be counted twice.
House only
The Senate's database blocks automated downloads. The Senate will be added later if a suitable data source is found.
