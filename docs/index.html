Following the stock trades of the U.S. Congress

Ekklesia Capital is a rule-based paper fund. It buys the stocks that members of the House of Representatives disclose buying, at the market open after the disclosure becomes public, and holds each one for six months.

Public information, used with discipline

Since 2012 the STOCK Act has required members of Congress to disclose their stock trades within 45 days. They sit close to legislation, hearings and budgets. Ekklesia turns their disclosures into a portfolio, with no human judgement in between.

The name comes from the ekklesia, the citizens' assembly of ancient Athens.

Transparent

All code, all data and every single decision are public on GitHub. Anyone can check why a stock was bought or skipped. This is one line from the decision log:

Bought  GOOGL, filed 12 Nov 2026
Price since the member's trade: +0.1% (limit +10%)
Order: market open, $1,000, sell after 6 months
Rule-based

The rules were fixed before the results were known. No gut feeling, no override and no tuning along the way.

Low cost

The planned management fee is 0.12% a year. Everything from reading a filing to placing an order runs automatically.

Independently reviewed

A separate review recomputed all 18,068 trades from the raw data and searched for look-ahead and survivorship bias over two rounds.

From filing to order, every trading day

The process runs on its own before the U.S. market opens. It can only act on what was public that day.

  1. Disclosure

    A member, or their spouse, trades a stock and reports it to the Clerk of the House.

    Legal deadline: 45 days

  2. Reading

    The House index and each PDF filing are downloaded and read. Scanned paper filings are skipped.

    Median delay: 28 days

  3. Filtering

    Only purchases of stocks and ETFs count. Options, dividend reinvestments and duplicates are removed.

    The member's own decisions

  4. Buying

    The stock is bought at the open the day after the filing, unless its price has already risen more than 10%.

    Skip above +10%

  5. Selling

    Each position is sold after six months. Larger disclosed purchases get a larger weight.

    Hold for 6 months

16.0% a year since 2016

+400%

From January 2016 to November 2026 the simulated strategy grew $100 to $500, against $438 for the S&P 500. The extra return came with larger swings, and most of the lead was built in 2020 to 2022.

What can go wrong

Market risk
The fund holds only U.S. stocks and moves closely with the market (beta 1.10). When the market falls, the fund usually falls further.
Concentration
Large disclosed purchases carry more weight, so a few members can dominate the portfolio for a while.
Delay
Trades become public a median of 28 days after they happen, and 19% are filed late. Any information edge may be gone before the fund buys.
Legislation
Bills have been introduced in Congress to restrict members from trading individual stocks. If one passes, the strategy loses its input.
Data
Scanned filings cannot be read automatically, filings can contain errors, and some delisted stocks are missing from the price data.
Currency
For an investor outside the U.S., returns also move with the dollar.

Questions

Does the strategy beat the market?

Over 2016 to 2026 it returned +400% against +338% for the S&P 500, or 16.0% a year against 14.6%. It was also more volatile, so adjusted for risk the index did slightly better (a Sharpe ratio of 0.80 against 0.87). The live paper fund is the real test.

Is it legal to trade on congressional stock disclosures?

The disclosures are public, and anyone can read them and trade on them for their own account. U.S. law does, however, prohibit using the reports for commercial purposes other than by news media. That is one reason Ekklesia is a paper fund and not a commercial product. This is not legal advice.

Why not buy on the member's trade date?

Because the trade only becomes known when the filing is published, a median of 28 days later. Using the trade date in a backtest would produce returns nobody could actually have earned.

What about following only the best members?

That was tested. Copying only the members with the best past results looked strong on paper, but most of it came from two members, and members who had done well did not keep doing well. Details are on the research page.

Why is the Senate not included?

The Senate's disclosure database blocks automated downloads. The Senate will be added in a later version if a suitable data source is found.

Who is behind it?

Ekklesia Capital is an independent research project, built in Python on public data from the House of Representatives and prices from Alpaca. It has no connection to Congress or to any asset manager.
